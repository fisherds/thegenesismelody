A Quick Foreword

Hey. Hi. Before we get started, I wanted to let you know that I love you. Yes, that’s extremely awkward to say in our generation, but it really shouldn’t be. Of course, I don’t know you, and we’ve never met. So my love for you is a bit limited, but you are an Image of God like me, so… I love you. Oops, I said it again.

My goal here is to help you see a pattern in Scripture that I think should be important to your life. We’re going to look mainly at Genesis 1-23. In these chapters God works with his human partners in many ways, but the structure of the text is very carefully designed. God creates and calls, those parts will be fun to study, but he also tests and brings about collapse in response to human partners. Trying to understand how God is at work even through a collapse should help give you hope and confidence in things yet to come, so those parts should not to be overlooked. This pattern has been woven into all scripture, and the more you study, the more you will see. Let’s become apprentices together so that we can know|love Him more.

Summary: What is The Genesis Melody?

When you approach the Bible, what do you see there? Well, what do you expect to see there? Over time we have been trained to see only some parts of the Bible. Our expectations are often at odds with our ability to simply receive the message. This guide will focus on reading Genesis 1-23 trying to see patterns. The goal is to approach the text as a blank slate. To be honest, I’m not capable of doing that either, but it’s important to know the mindset we’d like to have. Shaping the Bible to meet our expectations is rather pointless. The goal is to open ourselves up, so that we can be shaped by the Bible. I’m really excited to share with you some things I’ve gotten to see in Genesis. Through this writing process, I’ve felt the guiding presence of the Spirit myself and I’m just amazed at what these authors have been able to create.

The Hebrew Bible, the Old Testament, was carefully written by humans partnering with the work of the Spirit over the course of hundreds of years to make God known to us.1 As a result, it is a VERY dense, beautiful collection of ancient scrolls with many layers to unpack. Readers who start at the beginning, myself included, see only a small fraction of the depth the authors are communicating. However, the goal of this guide is not to learn everything. We’ll choose to focus on just one significant layer that is often overlooked.

There is a clear, repeated creation melody that runs throughout the Hebrew Bible and into the New Testament. Once you have eyes to see and ears to hear how the authors replay the notes, you won’t be able to miss them as you continue to read the Bible. This song starts with God bringing about creation, life, and blessing. He then calls human partners to share in the work. Often characteristic human choices lead to the division of relationships causing things to come to a head in a collapse. At other times, pattern breakers step in who choose to trust and obey God. Strangely, the collapse often falls on them just the same, but they go through the collapse with God. Much like the rain, which falls on the just and the unjust alike, they too experience genuine suffering, but through them God can once again bring about creation, life, and blessing from the non-life. Understanding how God continually brings about and sustains life is foundational not just in the Bible, but in our own lives as well. So the authors of the Bible provide a decoder ring, beginning on page one, to unlock the rest of the story.

Before we get started, I should explain the title. The word genesis means two things to us: first, it refers to the scroll of Genesis, but it also simply means what the word means. The word genesis comes from the Greek γένεσις (génesis), meaning “origin, source, beginning, or birth”. Matthew liked this word and so do I. So when choosing a title for a repeated creation pattern, a genesis, that is easily seen in the scroll of Genesis, it seemed only fitting to call it The Genesis Melody. However, don’t be fooled! The author of life keeps creating well beyond the pages of Genesis.

Why Study the Melody

What is the value in seeing the structure of the melody? The main focus in the Genesis courses is how the repeated notes should be ringing in our ears to link parts of the melody. Parallel stories might be separated by many chapters, but with only a few distinct notes the authors can put them side-by-side in your mind.2 One story informs the other, and both benefit from the connection. Let’s take a short but deep dive to consider one specific example: Genesis 3:6-7a. You probably know this text well. Notice the key words as you read it again.

God saw good, tov, טוב, in Genesis 1 seven times, ending in a “very good.” Humans, however, characteristically see what is good for themselves in their own eyes: we take and eat (an image to consume in this case), then give (send out non-life choices), as shown in 3:6 and 16:3. Structure

There are many notes in the melody as it develops, changes, and repeats, but we’ll focus on only the most fundamental four notes: Creation, Calling (Identity), Testing and Choices, and Collapse. The cycle goes around like a circle.

For the purposes of this guide, we will consider how this four-part melody is repeated seven times from Genesis 1 to 22. To be clear, the repeated pattern can be broken down in many ways and variations are welcome as we all understand the Biblical authors slightly differently, but the four most central notes of the melody seem to be:

  1. Creation - The transition from a state of non-life, chaos, situations-that-don’t-work into a state of life, order, and blessing. The gift of life and blessing to multiply. The process starts with the smallest presence of God as ruakh, the Holy Spirit, or the Word of God.
  2. Calling / Naming / Identification - God chooses to work with partners! These chosen humans are named, identified, or called to accomplish work in God’s rescue story. An anointed one or group of chosen people are identified and sent for a purpose. Rise up!
  3. Testing and Choices - This phase reveals the heart of the human partners. Will they trust God and build life or see what is good in their own eyes to consume.
  4. Collapse - Theoretically speaking, the melody should not require a collapse. Partners with God could always pass the test and use agreed upon healthy rhythms of reset like Sabbath cycles and Jubilee to avoid a collapse. However, practically speaking there is always a collapse. Choices that simply don’t work pile up to a breaking point. Sometimes humans are the spark. At other times, God initiates the collapse to wash away, but always for the purpose of future restoration. The forest has a fire, the trees suffer, and God chooses not to stop the fire, but to work with partners through it. These can be cosmic in scope, like the 10 plagues, the fall of Jerusalem, Exile, or the Flood, or personal, like Hagar, Ruth and Naomi, Stephen, or Jonah. But in every case, the important part is what happens next. God’s modus operandi is not a difficult pattern to spot. Even so, there are two perspectives to hold in tension. One asks, “Why would you fear something that God is fully in control of?” The other would say, “How can God allow his people to suffer?” Both are valid, and Scripture makes room for both, simultaneously trusting God while still crying out.

The longest phase of the melody is often Testing and Choices which can be many rounds of accumulating choices over generations. However, in some chapters it will be just a single really poor choice. Revealing the heart usually takes time and second chances are frequent. It is supposed to be an opportunity for growth as it happens, but depending on how human partners respond to their calling and make choices the cycle can follow one of two paths. The two paths are mentioned through the Bible, for example Matthew 7:13-14: “For wide is the gate and broad is the road that leads to destruction, and many enter through it. But small is the gate and narrow the road that leads to life, and only a few find it.” In this guide, we’ll call these paths: characteristic human choices or pattern breakers choices.

On the broad path there is no sense of receiving a calling, the calling is still there, but it is not received, so can be omitted. Then the choices focus on conceiving for yourself what is good. This leads to the division of relationships, and necessitates a pending collapse. However, God still works through the collapse.

The notes for the ‘characteristic human’ pattern are actually easier to spot in Genesis 1 to 22, as there are more humans making those choices. We will hear those notes again and again, so we will track those as the main pattern, but that doesn’t describe everyone.

Pattern Breakers!

Now, you might be thinking that this melody seems pretty gloom-and-doom. Everything ends in collapse! Is life really an inevitable downward spiral that ends in a collapse? That’s depressing! No, of course not. That is the characteristic human pattern, NOT the way to life. I think these authors love to make patterns just to break them. Let’s look at Genesis 5 for a moment which uses a very clear pattern. The text below is super long, but I bet you can see the point I’m making about pattern breaking in two seconds.

The point is NOT the pattern. The point is the pattern breaker! As we listen for the notes in the melody, be especially on the lookout for pattern breaker moments. Indeed, God is able to work through characteristic human choices, but see what God can do with the pattern breakers. These choices lead to life.

The partners who break the characteristic pattern do not avoid the collapse, but rather they receive their calling and go through the collapse with God. To be clear, it is the characteristic human choices that necessitate the collapse, but this group equally experiences the collapse. In fact, sometimes pattern breakers experience more suffering in this age. In response to that truth, it seems to me like God had two choices: take away free will or join us. What is impossible for man is possible with God, to go through a collapse. Pattern breakers listen to God’s voice, then get an opportunity to become partners in creation as God renews life not just in Genesis 1-23, but throughout the Bible. But I’m getting ahead of myself. First, we need to make sure we’re receiving the Word of God faithfully and representing the source of truth accurately. For example, this simple binary model of “some people are bad (characteristic human choices), some people are good (pattern breakers)” almost makes it seem like the pattern breakers have earned salvation. However, when you dive into the details of this text and see the complexity of humans, you will come to realize that God’s mercy is the driving factor in genesis, not humans. For example, in the fourth collapse it’s solely God’s chosen partners Avram and Sarai that cause the collapse, and God responds by blessing and renaming Avraham! When your deserve meter hits zero either individually or communally, cry out for mercy. There are no works by which you can save your own life anyway. Fortunately, in the melody, there are indeed times when God pulls someone kicking and screaming through the fire and into new life. Interestingly, though, long term that won’t work for people either, there is a necessary partnership in maintaining life. Genesis 1-7: The Melody Template

Instead of abstractly describing the characteristics of the notes, let’s look at the template of The Genesis Melody provided in Genesis 1-7. These early chapters of Genesis are intentionally placed at the start of this massive collection as an introduction to the entire work. They will plant seeds for themes that will take volumes to unpack. If we allow them to properly shape our imagination and world view, we will become better Bible readers.

Through prayer, meditation, study, and time the biblical authors have shared God’s message with us, but work is also necessary to receive it today. How would Jesus have expressed this point? In John 4:37-38 Jesus quotes an unusual phrase: “Thus the saying ‘One sows and another reaps’ is true... Others have done the hard work, and you have reaped the benefits of their labor.” Parables can have more than one meaning, a common Hebrew Bible technique, but, for our theme study, the seed sown represents the Wisdom of God planted in His Word. These authors have worked with the Spirit to plant the key insights, but communication requires work from both the speaker and the listener. We have to put in time together with the Spirit to convert words into a relationship, so that we can live in a right relationship with Him. The authors use analogies that we can understand, like seed or light, but it all centers around taking on God’s view of reality as truth. The only way to reap this freely given fruit is by meditating on it over time, then living it out in our lives. Let’s get started by looking for the melody in Genesis 1-7 using the table below.

Melody Cycle 1 Creation

Genesis
1:1 - 2:3
plus 2:4-15

After the seven-word Hebrew title of Genesis 1:1, we read about the precreation state of watery chaos, a standard image for nothing at that point in history. However, with the presence of Elohim about to act, hovering as Ruakh over the face of the waters, we have hope. Elohim speaks to create light and divides those chaos waters to bring order to the sea, sky, and land in Days 1-3. He then fills those spaces with inhabitants in Days 4-6, starting with the lights in the sky, which are called signs. He makes living beings to fill those spaces, giving them a blessing to be fruitful and multiply. Ultimately, God creates the human in His image, male and female He created them, which He sees as “very good.” Finally, on the 7th day, God rests, i.e., takes up residence and rules without conflict. In the imagination of the Ancient Near East, when a ruler rests, it is a time of shalom, peace within the land. We know this story well, thinking of it as a one-time creation event, but we need to keep reading to realize that creation is a cycle. It will repeat!3


Still in the Creation section, Genesis 2:4-6 offers another perspective of non-life, a barren desert without water. God brings about life first via a stream, then the human, and finally trees, all in fun Hebrew wordplay. Again, God’s life is present, gently breathing the breath of life into the human.

Melody Cycle 1 Calling

Genesis
1:27 and 2:15

The calling in the first round of the melody is the most significant in the Bible. Every human is called an Image of God, the Imago Dei in Latin. That identification is used as a present tense, you are right now an Image of God, but that’s a surprising phrase. It is the true identity of every human, but I don’t personally feel like we’ve fully received that calling yet. It reminds me of Ephesians 2:6, where Paul says that God has already “raised us up with him and seated us with him in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus”. It is a calling that is true, but I think God sees me differently than I see myself. The text then goes on to call us rulers over the entire cosmos that God has created. Receive ownership of all that is good. Woah!


Then in the Eden creation narrative there is a calling there as well. The human is called to work (עבד ‘avad) and to keep (שמר shamar) the Garden in Eden. These are words that will later describe the priests who work and keep the temple. If only we had a kingdom of priests to represent God to the nations.

Melody Cycle 1 Choice

Genesis 2:16 - 3:24

The Choice portion of the melody starts with the divine command not to eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and bad. However, between the divine command and the Fall, the authors insert an example of Yahweh seeing, taking, building, and giving in 2:18-22. Unlike Yahweh, the humans see what is good in their eyes, then take, eat, and give non-life. The inevitable consequences follow. God acknowledges the unfortunate new state of His creation but makes some promises worth noting on the way out, see Genesis 3:15.

Melody Cycle 1 Division

Genesis
4:1 - 4:24, 6:1 - 6:12

Outside of Eden, future generations escalate the spiral of Division in the characteristic human cycle. As predicted in Genesis 3:16, they conceive for themselves instead of receiving from God, then experience great anguish. Cain kills Abel. Lamech takes two wives and murders a boy for striking him. The Sons of Elohim see and take the beautiful, tov, daughters of humanity in a parallel spiritual rebellion. We end up with the Nephilim (the fallen ones), and the whole land is ruined by Genesis 6:12. If Genesis 1 had been a singular creation event then the whole cosmos would have been a very short, rather pointless, project. Fortunately for us, creation, life, and blessing are repeated notes in God’s song.

Melody Cycle 1 Collapse

Genesis
6:13 - 7:24

The Collapse here is one we all know well. God brings about a great leveling act to cleanse the land via the Flood.6 This is a clear return to the Genesis 1:2 precreation state. By the end of Genesis 7, the world is again in the same watery chaos image of nothing, minus one small difference… a boat, covered with atonement, preserving seed. This symbol of God preserving life is of no small significance for you and me. Noah, seven other people, and a whole bunch of animals sit and wait until Yahweh does it again. “How long? Oh LORD?” the poets say. How long until You do it again?

Genesis 1-7: Pattern Breakers!

In the same way that Genesis 1-7 is the template for the characteristic humans, it is also the template for pattern breakers! Clearly, Enoch and Noah don’t follow the characteristic division and collapse cycle. Their patterns will be clear and discussed elsewhere, but the most commonly overlooked pattern breaker in this cycle is the delightful ski jump at the end of chapter 4. There is a moment where the rapid division descent takes a sharp up turn. Everyone reads the words, but we often overlook the significance of this pattern breaking moment. From 4:1 to 4:24 we see characteristic human choices to establish “normal” human behavior. In that section, Eve claims to have made a man all by herself in comparison to Yahweh. The authors use clever wordplay by noting only that Cain was conceived, the same Hebrew root word as heron (הרנך) from 3:16, but with no mention of their male child being called a son, the Hebrew word ben (בן). Cain, however, is invited by God to be a pattern breaker in the next generation. He can rule over sin, but instead he too chooses the broad path of non-life. Then Cain’s wife will also conceive, but again no mention of their child, a different Enoch, being a son. We have already seen where that path spirals out of control in the division section above. Let’s skip to verse 25, where we will see something new that you won’t find on the division spiral.

In the closing section of chapter 4, verses 25 and 26, we start with intentionally repeated words from verse 1, “Adam knew his wife again,” but things go differently this time!

First, right at this pivot, the Hebrew name for Adam changes from The Adam, i.e. “The Human”, to just Adam, a personal name. Conversely, Eve’s name was used in verse 1, but now she called only “his wife” to emphasize their union over individuality. Next, the authors hint that something is new by only stating that she bears a son (yalad ben) with no mention of her conceiving. So it’s the same, but also opposite in every subtle way. Interesting.

4:1 Now, the human knew his wife Eve,

and she conceived and she gave birth


4:25 and Adam knew his wife again

and she gave birth to a son,

To be clear, she did conceive for every birth. She clearly became pregnant at some point, but the authors do not mention it, since this birth is not all about Eve. Finally, Adam’s wife shows her new perspective by giving the glory to Elohim, not herself. She sees life as a gift to be received instead of an act accomplished by herself. Then it hits you... she is the first to break the pattern! She was the first to take the fruit, but she was also the first to turn back and make a different choice. We all fail, that’s really nothing new, but we don’t all turn back to God. Eve, Mother of all living-ones, is famous for Genesis 3:6-7, but she was also the first to receive the fruit. Welcome home! I say “Welcome home” because Eden is a place where heaven and earth overlap. A place where human and divine work together as one (yachad in Hebrew). It has always been more than just a physical spot on the map. Let’s bring life to the next generation together!

Genesis 8-11: The Second Round of the Melody

Before we jump into the second cycle of the melody, I’d like to pause for a moment and congratulate you for making it to the center of the center of this guide. We find ourselves in Genesis chapter 8, section 8 of this guide, and it feels symbolic, as if very early in the morning on the first day of a new week, the dawn of new creation. It’s Sunday morning! Genesis 1 will replay in Genesis 8:1-9:18, but in new ways for a new generation. A pattern is not a pattern unless it repeats. So in Genesis 1-11, the introduction to the Bible, we see the cycle of life, not once, but twice. The Hebrew Bible authors are quietly going to reshape the way you see the world. The Spirit has breathed into them a truth that God never stops bringing creation, life, and blessing. Let’s reflect on their words and try to become listeners that live out Psalm 1:1-1:3.

The cycle begins again in Genesis 8:1, unmistakably, right in the chiastic center of the flood narrative.8 Life begins with the presence of God, shown again as Ruakh (רוח), the Hebrew word that means wind, breath, or presence.

Melody Cycle 2 Choice

Genesis 9:19-29

The next generation repeats the sins of the old as the Choice notes begin to play in the new Eden space. Noah takes the wine in an illicit way by getting drunk. Then he uncovers himself in the middle of his tent. These are verbal echoes of Adam and Eve taking the fruit in the middle of the garden, then realizing they were naked. Finally, Ham “saw the nakedness of his father”. In Hebrew, a man’s nakedness is his wife, Leviticus 18:7-8 spells this out plainly. The act is so terrible that the authors use a common Hebrew euphemism. Despite our modern debates and mystery, “seeing the nakedness” is a standard phrase stating that Ham had sex with his father’s wife (if you would like to have more evidence go read Ezekiel 22:10 in a literal translation). The word choice ties this unthinkable moment into the “seeing what is good in his eyes” theme. Translators face an impossible task here. They need to maintain the “seeing” hyperlink, discern the idioms, and keep it to five words or less. So they usually just follow the pattern of prior translations, throw their hands in the air, and give a literal word-for-word translation, leaving interpretation to the reader (which is fine, let commentaries explain it). Next, much like God did in the Garden exit, this new state results in Noah giving blessings to Yahweh the Elohim of Shem and curses to Canaan. Wait, Canaan? Where did Ham go? It opens with a title in Genesis 11:27, “Now these are the generations of Terakh.” Then, following the Genesis 1 pattern, we have a new precreation state in 11:28-32. This time, the symbols for non-life are not watery chaos or a dry desert. Instead, if you read with eyes looking for symbols of death and disorder, you will notice:

  • And Terakh died in Haran

Without God’s presence, the “wild and waste” from Genesis 1:2 is depicted with these new symbols of precreation non-life. The collapse opens with a clear title in 22:1 stating that Avraham is tested. When you think “test,” try to imagine an experience that reveals the truth, not a stressful exam with right and wrong answers. It reveals what is already present through action. For example, Noah was called righteous in 6:9 by the narrator, but he still had to actually build the Ark in 6:22 and Yahweh sees the truth in 7:1. Likewise, Avraham was called righteous in 15:6, but he would still have to go through his test in Genesis 22. God is giving him a quest to reveal truth, not tempting him to do evil. There is a difference. God is asking him to trust in His wisdom beyond anything he can see with his eyes. It is clearly not a test God would give you or me, but the test fits the man if you reflect on Avraham’s whole story. Jesus illustrated that tests are specific to the person when he spoke to the rich young ruler. That man’s test was based on the thing that separated him from God, his wealth. He needed to trust God and give away all he had to the poor, then follow Jesus. In his case, it only revealed his inability to take a radical step of trust, and he went away downcast. When God chose “Father Abraham” to initiate His restoration plan, he knew exactly what test would reveal him and he knew that Avraham would trust his voice. In the same way, The Father knew what test would reveal The Son and he trusted the Son to complete it. That test reveals God to us.9 Consider Psalm 22:31 and John 3:16.

The hyperlinks from Genesis 22 to Jesus are impossible to miss. It is a test for both the father and the son. However, they traveled together perfectly aligned, both before and after all the details are known. The phrase “and the two of them went, together-as-one” (yachad in Hebrew) is repeated three times. Isaac put the wood on his back and walked with his father at the same physical place where Jesus will later be lifted up on the cross. Keep in mind that all of this was written hundreds of years before Jesus ever walked the earth. That’s worth a long walk and a cup of tea. Finally, Avraham’s collapse and Jesus’ collapse end the same way, the son is returned to life. Avraham is revealed. Elohim sees. And a going-up offering of a ram substitutes for Avraham’s offspring. As always, God makes sure that creation, life, and blessing have the final say over a collapse.

Before we leave this story, let’s consider what it might have meant to Jesus. What thoughts raced through Jesus’ head as he mentally recounted this story in the Garden of Gethsemane? And when did He fully grasp the details? John the Baptist publicly named Jesus as the “Lamb of God” in John 1:29. I think Jesus knew fully what that meant. Yahweh’s character has been known since Exodus 34:6-7, but Jesus would still have to go through the test. This plan, made before time began, is how God would reveal who He is to us. God loves you. He has said it in His Word, and He has shown it in His actions. He has passed the test to show that nothing will separate Him from joining us even when he knows it’s coming.

Then the outpouring of blessings given by God’s oath “on the heel” of the test is worth reading a few times. Genesis 22:16-18, Yahweh’s Oath (שבע sheva) at the conclusion of melody seven (שבע sheva):

Let’s read it again, “all nations will find blessing in your seed.” The author wants you to remember Genesis 3:15 by mentioning the words “heel” and the future “seed” so close together. Two words are more than enough to see this connection.

Seeing the Melody in the New Testament

The Genesis Melody is about a time when God sees (or sometimes causes) a collapse and instead of it being the end, it is an opportunity to bring about a new creation, a genesis.

We’ll briefly pause our review of Genesis and consider The Genesis Melody as it plays out in the Gospel According to Luke. You hopefully know this cycle of the melody well.3 Jesus, being the monogenes (μονογενής), is utterly unique, but, sure enough, He fulfills the same melody pattern. In Luke, the story opens with God’s Spirit creating life in two impossible places of non-life: a barren womb long past childbearing and one that had never known a man. Luke starts, not just with the birth of Jesus to the virgin Mary, but with the interwoven story of John the Baptist’s birth to Zechariah and Elizabeth. The author of this story saw that even the names of the parents were significant:

  • Zechariah, from the Hebrew Zakar-Yah זכריה, means “Yahweh Remembered, zakar.”8 It’s that zakar word again, the one that means “acts on his promise.” Compare that with Genesis 8:1, “Elohim remembered, zakar, Noah”8 or 19:29, “Yahweh remembered, zakar, Avraham.”
  • Elizabeth, from the Hebrew Eli-Sheva אלישבע, means “My Elohim’s Oath”. It’s that sheva, oath, word again! Compare that with 22:16, “I swear an oath (שבע sheva).”

Zechariah and Elizabeth are set up as clear mirrors of Avraham and Sarah, childless at a good old age. And if you put their names together, you literally have “Yahweh Remembered His Oath from Genesis 22!” OK, I admit, I added that last bit, but “Zechariah Elizabeth” means that Yahweh made good, delivered on, remembered his oath to Abraham in Jesus and John was the forerunner of that good news.

The authors who handed over source material to the gentile Luke, see Luke 1:1, saw the Genesis melody at work. They partnered with Luke to share the Good News about Jesus to all the nations via the same creation notes that echo Genesis, now appearing in Luke, chapters 1 and 2. Jesus is then clearly called (identified) at John’s Baptism. Luke’s story then has a choice in the wilderness for forty days and a matching test in Gethsemane before the end. There is a journey with Jesus, just like Avram’s journey with Yahweh. Jesus and the Father are fully aligned, fully unified, completely faithful, the only ever, perfect Image of God. However, in his generation there was still a division with the leaders of Israel (that’s putting it lightly). Then a replay of the seventh cycle collapse at the cross.6 Finally, this is the most important part, what comes after a collapse? The author of life allows His own body, His Son, to experience collapse, from which God will… You know exactly what happened next, on the third day no less, see Genesis 22:4. The Genesis Melody has been written in Scripture for a thousand years. Jesus surely said many similar things on the road to Emmaus in Luke 24, as he “opened the Scriptures” to his disciples.3

Beyond Genesis 22: After the Sheva Cycle

The melody in Genesis, of course, doesn’t end in chapter 22. If you read Matthew 24:1-14, it’s clear Jesus knew the inevitable path of typical human choices across every generation, and he prepared his disciples for what was coming. The cycle would not end even with Jesus’ resurrection. Something did change that day, but the difference is not what you would see with your eyes. Hearts aren’t changed in our world today either, so every generation, including ours, faces the same challenges. Yahweh, the one who IS, has always known the answer, and He has made it known to us through His Son, completing a test, revealing the truth. God really loves you.

After the mountaintop, you should also read chapter 23. It is a wonderfully symbolic story about paying the full price for the land. You can meditate with me about why that is important. How does that square with Yahweh promising to just give the land in Genesis 12:7 and 17:8 (or Ezekiel 11:17)? Shouldn’t we just receive the free Eden gift? Anyway, the story keeps going after Genesis 23. The eighth melody cycle, 22:20-25:18, ends with Avraham’s death and the scattering of his various sons. He takes a third wife and has six more sons in chapter 25 (oh my). The biblical authors aren’t selling “happily ever after” stories; they are communicating the choices we humans make and how God works through it all (consider Jacob!). After Avraham is old and complete (sheva), he is “gathered to his people” in 25:8. The melody, however, continues generation after generation with Rivqah (Rebecca) and Yitskhaq (Isaac), then Jacob and Esau, Judah and Joseph, and on into our lives.

Extra Sheva Observations

To bring this discussion full circle, let’s return to Genesis 21-22 once more to see evidence that these authors, with the help of the Spirit, already realized the significance of this forward pointing message.

Throughout Scripture the authors regularly highlight the most important concept of a literary unit by using key repeated words, known as lead words. In this seventh cycle of the melody, which goes from the voice in the sky bringing life to Hagar and Ishmael, through the binding of Isaac, the primary lead word is clearly sheva. The same Hebrew letters of sheva, שבע, form multiple Hebrew words. Think of it like the English letters lead, which has multiple meanings: lead the way, pencil lead, or leader. The Hebrew word sheva, שבע, means seven, oath, and, later, completion, making it a very significant word. Perhaps you’ve noticed the number 7 gets used a lot. The repeated use of sheva in this melody cycle is another cairn left by the authors to reinforce that we are on the right track. It gives us confidence that we are indeed seeing the path that these authors laid out for us.

The sheva theme is everywhere in this melody cycle. The creation notes open with Hagar and Ishmael at Beer-Sheva, the Well of Seven. The middle section with Avimelek takes place at Beer-Sheva, centered on an oath, a sheva. Then, after the mountain in Moriah, the literary unit ends with Avraham dwelling in Beer-Sheva. The start, middle, and end are all at Beer-Sheva. Everything points to Yahweh’s culminating sheva, His oath. In addition to the location, there are more sheva words used in Genesis 21-22 than you might guess. The numbers tell the story:

In fact if you focus on only the use of sheva words in cycles three to seven with Avraham it becomes clear that the authors have intentionally marked this round of the melody as the Sheva Cycle.

By modern writing standards, the repeated use of sheva borders on excessive, but that’s exactly the point. Listeners would hear the word as prominent and realize this is the seven-oath for completion. Imagine sitting in the temple courts on Shabbat listening to Simeon read this text. The word sheva appears nine times as this cycle builds toward the collapse, and then the 10th is Yahweh’s Sheva, His oath, the very first words of the new creation (plus two more Beer-Sheva uses to finish the literary unit). Did ancient readers like Simeon emphasize the word each time for his hearers?

Let’s look at one example from Genesis 21:28-32, a “seemingly” insignificant passage.

The authors use extra sheva, שבע, words surrounding being a witness. That’ll preach! In this passage with Avraham and Avimelek, two men whose names mean Father of Nations and My Father is King, make the first human-to-human covenant in the Bible. They mend the division cycle without directly mentioning God using a covenant. That’s interesting! The testing and the collapse are still coming in Genesis 22, Abraham has made a lifetime of choices that need resolution, but we see a glimmer of hope in chapter 21 about how to slow down this crazy hamster wheel… covenant relationships with fellow images of God. Covenants aren’t as good as changed hearts, but usually it’s the best option we have available outside of Eden. Every generation has to renew a covenant, Isaac and Avimelek in Genesis 26, Jacob and Lavan in Genesis 31, and on into today. This seventh cycle is packed with insights!
